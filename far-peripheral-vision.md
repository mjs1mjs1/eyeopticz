<p class="lead">The human visual field extends beyond 90 degrees to the side, yet vision past about 60 degrees has hardly been researched. Calculations suggest that an intraocular lens reduces far peripheral vision, but it is almost never measured.</p>

## In brief

- Clinical visual field tests and most research concentrate on the central part of the field.
- A limiting visual angle of about **105 degrees** was reported in 1915. Modern clinical equipment generally measures to **90 degrees at most**.
- How far the field extends depends on the optics at the front of the eye, including the iris and pupil.
- After cataract surgery the natural lens is replaced by a much smaller intraocular lens. Light at very wide angles can pass beside it, which changes what the far periphery sees.

## Why it matters

Peripheral vision is used for noticing movement, moving around safely and driving, but it is seldom tested at its outer limits. Because so little is measured, changes caused by eye surgery can go unnoticed. One visible example is [negative dysphotopsia]({{ '/negative-dysphotopsia/' | relative_url }}), a dark shadow that some people see at the edge of their vision after cataract surgery.

The [nodal point]({{ '/nodal-point/' | relative_url }}) gives a simple way to relate wide angles in the world to positions on the retina, which makes this part of the field easier to model.
