<p class="lead">After cataract surgery, some people see a dark shadow at the far edge of their vision. This is called negative dysphotopsia. The likely cause is that the intraocular lens is much smaller than the natural lens it replaces. The shadow is a property of how the eye forms images, not a real shadow.</p>

<p class="note">This page explains optics research. It is not medical advice. If you have concerns about your vision, please see an ophthalmologist or optometrist.</p>

## In brief

- In the far periphery, some light passes through the intraocular lens and some passes beside it.
- These two paths form separate images on the retina. Between them there can be a gap that receives less light, which is seen as a dark shadow.
- How noticeable the shadow is depends on factors such as pupil size and eye rotation, which the simulations below show.
- **Positive dysphotopsia** is different. It is bothersome light effects, often at night, caused by reflection inside the edge of the lens.

## Simulations

These videos simulate what may make the peripheral shadow bothersome.

### Eye rotation

The eye rotates to the right and back. It starts with a 2.5 mm pupil, and the pupil increases to 3, 3.5, 4 and 5 mm during the rotations.

### Pupil diameter change

The pupil starts at 4 mm while the eye makes a small rotation right and back. The pupil then drops suddenly to 2.5 mm and the rotation repeats. The sequence runs three times.
